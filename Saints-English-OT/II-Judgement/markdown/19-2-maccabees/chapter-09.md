## Text

**9:1** Now about that time, Antiochus happened to be retreating in disorder from the regions of Persia.

**9:2** For he had entered the city called Persepolis and attempted to rob the temples and to hold the city in subjection; therefore the multitudes rushed out and turned to the aid of arms, and it happened that Antiochus was put to flight by the inhabitants and made a disgraceful retreat.

**9:3** And while he was at Ecbatana, word reached him of what had happened to Nicanor and to the forces of Timothy.

**9:4** Carried away with fury, he thought to vent upon the Jews even the injury done by those who had put him to flight; therefore he commanded his charioteer to drive without ceasing and complete the journey, though the judgment from heaven was already accompanying him. For he had spoken thus arrogantly: "I will make Jerusalem a common burial ground of the Jews when I arrive there."

**9:5** But the all-seeing Lord, the God of Israel, struck him with an incurable and unseen blow; for no sooner had he ceased speaking than an irremediable pain in his bowels seized him, and bitter torments of his inward parts,

**9:6** most justly, since he had tortured the inward parts of others with many and strange afflictions.

**9:7** Yet he by no means ceased from his insolence, but was still filled with arrogance, breathing fire in his rage against the Jews and commanding the journey to be hastened. And it happened that he also fell from his chariot as it sped swiftly along, and suffering a grievous fall, every limb of his body was racked and wrenched.

**9:8** Thus he who in superhuman arrogance presumed to rival the waves of the sea, and supposed he could weigh the heights of the mountains in a balance, was brought down to the ground and carried in a litter, displaying to all the manifest power of God;

**9:9** so that worms even swarmed from the body of the impious man, and while he was still alive in agony and pain his flesh fell away, and by his stench the whole army was oppressed by his decay.

**9:10** And the man who a little while before thought he could touch the stars of heaven, no one could bear to carry because of the intolerable burden of the stench.

**9:11** There, then, broken as he was, he began to cease from the greatness of his arrogance and to come to his senses under the divine scourge, as his pains intensified every moment.

**9:12** And when he could no longer endure even his own stench, he said these words: "It is right to be subject to God, and for one who is mortal not to think arrogant thoughts."

**9:13** Then the wretch prayed to the Sovereign Lord, who would no longer have mercy on him, declaring thus:

**9:14** that he would proclaim free the holy city, which he had been hastening to level to the ground and establish as a common burial ground;

**9:15** and that the Jews, whom he had resolved not even to deem worthy of burial, but to destroy along with their infants as food for birds and wild beasts, he would make all of them equal to the Athenians;

**9:16** and that he would adorn with the finest votive offerings the holy temple which he had previously plundered, and restore all the sacred vessels many times over, and supply from his own revenues the expenses required for the sacrifices;

**9:17** and in addition to these things, that he would also become a Jew and go through every inhabited place proclaiming the mighty power of God.

**9:18** Yet when his sufferings by no means ceased, for the righteous judgment of God had come upon him, he despaired of his condition and wrote to the Jews the letter transcribed below, in the form of a supplication, containing these words:

**9:19** "To the worthy Jewish citizens, hearty greetings, good health, and prosperity, from Antiochus, King and General.

**9:20** May you be well, and your children; and your own affairs shall be according to your desire. Placing my hope in heaven,

**9:21** I remembered your honor and goodwill with deep affection. Having returned from the regions of Persia and fallen into a troublesome illness, I have judged it necessary to take thought for the common security of you all,

**9:22** not despairing of my own condition, but having great hope that I shall escape from this illness.

**9:23** Yet considering that my father also, at the times when he marched into the upper regions, appointed his successor,

**9:24** so that if anything unexpected should happen or anything grievous should come to pass, the people in the country, knowing to whom the affairs of state had been left, might not be thrown into confusion;

**9:25** and observing in addition that the neighboring rulers and those bordering the kingdom are watching for opportunities and awaiting what will happen, I have appointed my son Antiochus as king, whom I often entrusted and commended to most of you when I hurried up to the upper satrapies; and I have written to him what is set forth below.

**9:26** Therefore I urge and request you, remembering the benefits conferred both publicly and privately, that each of you preserve your present goodwill toward me and my son;

**9:27** for I am persuaded that he, being aligned with my purpose, will deal with you mildly and humanely."

**9:28** So the murderer and blasphemer, having suffered the worst torments, just as he had dealt with others, ended his life by a most pitiable death in a foreign land among the mountains.

**9:29** And Philip, his foster brother, escorted the body home; then, fearing the son of Antiochus, he crossed over into Egypt to Ptolemy Philometor.

## Translator Notes

- **9:8** — The Greek source text reads antidokon without a following infinitive ("presumed to rival"), whereas other manuscripts read arti dokon ... epitassein ("who just now thought he could command").
- **9:20** — The Greek source text preserves a shorter greeting here, omitting the conditional "if" and the clause of thanksgiving to God found in other witnesses.
