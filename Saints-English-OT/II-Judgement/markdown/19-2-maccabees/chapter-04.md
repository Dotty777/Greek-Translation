## Text

**4:1** Now the aforementioned Simon, who had become an informer regarding the money and his homeland, spoke evil of Onias, claiming that he was the one who had incited Heliodorus and had been the contriver of the evils.

**4:2** And the benefactor of the city, the protector of his countrymen, and a zealot for the laws, he dared to call a conspirator against public affairs.

**4:3** As the enmity advanced to such a degree that murders were even committed by one of those approved by Simon,

**4:4** Onias, perceiving the peril of the contention, and seeing that Apollonius the son of Menestheus, the governor of Coele-Phoenicia and Syria, was increasing the malice of Simon,

**4:5** presented himself before the king, not becoming an accuser of his fellow citizens, but looking to what was beneficial both publicly and privately for the entire multitude.

**4:6** For he saw that without royal attention it was impossible for public affairs to obtain peace, and that Simon would not cease from his folly.

**4:7** Now when Seleucus departed this life, and Antiochus, who was called Epiphanes, received the kingdom, Jason the brother of Onias corruptly usurped the high priesthood,

**4:8** promising the king in a petition three hundred and sixty talents of silver, and eighty talents from another source of revenue.

**4:9** In addition to these, he undertook to pay another one hundred and fifty talents, if it were granted to him by his authority to establish a gymnasium and a youth corps for himself, and to enroll those in Jerusalem as Antiochians.

**4:10** When the king consented and he gained control of the office, he immediately shifted his countrymen over to the Greek manner of life.

**4:11** And setting aside the royal privileges established for the Jews through John, the father of Eupolemus, who made the embassy for friendship and alliance with the Romans, and abolishing the lawful institutions, he introduced unlawful customs.

**4:12** For he gladly established a gymnasium directly beneath the citadel itself, and bringing the foremost of those in our time into subjection, he led them under the Greek hat.

**4:13** Now such was the height of Hellenism and the advance of foreign ways, because of the surpassing profanity of the ungodly Jason, who was no high priest,

**4:14** that the priests were no longer eager for the services of the altar; rather, despising the temple and neglecting the sacrifices, they hastened to partake of the unlawful provision in the wrestling arena after the summons of the discus,

**4:15** holding the ancestral honors as nothing, while regarding the Greek glories as the finest.

**4:16** For this very reason, a grievous calamity surrounded them; and the very ones whose ways of life they emulated, and whom they desired to resemble in every respect, they had as their enemies and avengers.

**4:17** For it is no light matter to act ungodly against the divine laws; yet the time that follows will declare these things.

**4:18** Now when the games held every fifth year were being celebrated in Tyre, and the king was present,

**4:19** the vile Jason sent envoys, as Antiochians from Jerusalem, carrying three hundred drachmas of silver for the sacrifice of Heracles; yet those who carried them requested that they not be used for sacrifice,

**4:20** but, on account of those who were present, for the construction of the triremes.

**4:21** Now when Apollonius the son of Menestheus was sent into Egypt for the enthronement ceremonies of King Philometor, Antiochus, learning that he was hostile to his own established affairs, took thought for his own security; therefore, having come to Joppa, he proceeded to Jerusalem.

**4:22** And being magnificently received by Jason and the city, he was welcomed in with torchlight processions and shouts; and after that, he marched his army into Phoenicia.

**4:23** Now after a period of three years, Jason sent Menelaus, the brother of the aforementioned Simon, to convey the money to the king and to complete memoranda concerning necessary matters.

**4:24** But he, having been presented to the king and having magnified him with the appearance of authority, diverted the high priesthood to himself, outbidding Jason by three hundred talents of silver.

**4:25** Having received the royal mandates, he arrived, bringing nothing worthy of the high priesthood, but possessing the fury of a cruel tyrant and the rage of a savage beast.

**4:26** So Jason, who had corruptly undermined his own brother, being himself undermined by another, was driven as a fugitive into the Ammonite country.

**4:27** Now Menelaus held fast to the office, yet of the money promised to the king he made no regular payment,

**4:28** although Sostratus, the commander of the citadel, demanded payment—for to him belonged the collection of the revenues. For this reason, the two of them were summoned by the king.

**4:29** And Menelaus left his own brother Lysimachus as deputy of the high priesthood, while Sostratus left Crates, who was over the Cyprians.

**4:30** While these matters were taking place, it came to pass that the people of Tarsus and Mallus revolted, because they were being given as a gift to Antiochis, the king's concubine.

**4:31** Therefore the king went in haste to settle affairs, leaving Andronicus, one of those held in high rank, as his deputy.

**4:32** Now Menelaus, supposing that he had found a favorable opportunity, stole certain golden vessels belonging to the temple and presented them to Andronicus, while others he had already sold to Tyre and the surrounding cities.

**4:33** And when Onias clearly learned of this, he reported it, having withdrawn to a place of sanctuary at Daphne, which lies near Antioch.

**4:34** Therefore Menelaus took Andronicus aside privately and urged him to do away with Onias; and Andronicus came to Onias, and having yielded to deceit and exchanged pledges with oaths, giving his right hand, even though he was regarded with suspicion, he persuaded him to come forth from the sanctuary, and immediately put him to death, having no reverence for justice.

**4:35** For this reason not only the Jews, but also many of the other nations, were indignant and deeply grieved at the unjust murder of the man.

**4:36** And when the king returned from the regions of Cilicia, the Jews in the city appealed to him, with the Greeks also sharing their hatred of the wickedness, because Onias had been slain without cause.

**4:37** Therefore Antiochus was grieved in his soul and moved to pity, and wept because of the self-control and great orderly conduct of the departed man;

**4:38** and inflamed with wrath, he immediately stripped off the purple robe of Andronicus and tore off his garments, and having led him around throughout the whole city to the very place where he had committed impiety against Onias, there he put the murderer out of the way, the Lord rendering to him the punishment he deserved.

**4:39** Now when many acts of sacrilege had been committed in the city by Lysimachus with the consent of Menelaus, and the report had spread abroad, the multitude gathered together against Lysimachus, for many golden vessels had already been carried away.

**4:40** And as the crowds were stirred up and filled with wrath, Lysimachus armed about three thousand men and began an unjust assault, led by a certain Auranus, a man advanced in years and no less advanced in ignorance.

**4:41** But perceiving the attack of Lysimachus, some snatched up stones, others thick pieces of wood, and some grabbed handfuls of the ashes lying nearby, and hurled them in confusion at those with Lysimachus.

**4:42** For this reason they wounded many of them, struck down some, and drove all of them into flight; and the temple robber himself they slew beside the treasury.

**4:43** Now concerning these matters, a trial was brought against Menelaus.

**4:44** And when the king arrived at Tyre, the three men sent by the council of elders presented their case before him.

**4:45** Now Menelaus, being already defeated, promised a considerable sum of money to Ptolemy the son of Dorymenes, to persuade the king.

**4:46** Therefore Ptolemy took the king aside into a colonnade, as if to refresh himself, and changed his mind.

**4:47** So he acquitted Menelaus, the cause of all the wickedness, of the charges brought against him; while those unfortunate men—who, even if they had pleaded before Scythians, would have been acquitted as blameless—he condemned to death.

**4:48** Swiftly, therefore, they suffered the unjust penalty—those who had spoken on behalf of the city, the peoples, and the sacred vessels.

**4:49** For this reason even the Tyrians, detesting the wickedness, magnificently provided what was needed for their burial.

**4:50** But Menelaus, through the covetousness of those in power, remained in office, growing in wickedness and standing forth as a great conspirator against his fellow citizens.

## Translator Notes

- **4:4** — The Greek source reads "Coele-Phoenicia and Syria" (Koiles Phoinikes kai Syrias) rather than the more common "Coele-Syria and Phoenicia," and is rendered literally here.
- **4:12** — The Greek source reads "those in our time" (ton eph' hemon) rather than the variant "the youths" (ton ephebon). "Under the Greek hat" renders hypo petason, referring to the broad-brimmed hat worn by Greek gymnasium youths.
- **4:19** — The Greek source specifies three hundred drachmas of silver and moves directly into verse 20 without the longer transitional clause found in some manuscripts regarding the impropriety of sacrifice and the sender's intent.
- **4:33** — The Greek source reads apenegken ("reported" or "brought charges") rather than the textual variant apelegchen ("exposed" or "rebuked").
- **4:40** — The Greek text names the leader Auranus (some witnesses read Tyrannus) and describes him as advanced in "ignorance" (agnoian) rather than the variant "folly" (anoian).
