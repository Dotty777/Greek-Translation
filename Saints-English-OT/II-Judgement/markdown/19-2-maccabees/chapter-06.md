## Text

**6:1** Not long afterward, the king sent an Athenian elder to compel the Jews to depart from the laws of their fathers and no longer to live by the laws of God;

**6:2** and also to defile the temple in Jerusalem and call it the temple of Olympian Zeus, and to call the one at Gerizim—just as those who dwelt in the place were—the temple of Zeus the Friend of Strangers.

**6:3** Now the onset of this wickedness was harsh and altogether grievous to bear.

**6:4** For the temple was filled with debauchery and reveling by the Gentiles, who caroused with harlots and had intercourse with women within the sacred courts, and moreover brought inside things that were not lawful.

**6:5** And the altar was filled with abominable things forbidden by the laws.

**6:6** Neither was it possible to keep the Sabbath, nor to observe the feasts of their fathers, nor simply to confess oneself to be a Jew.

**6:7** And with bitter compulsion they drove them on the king's monthly birthday to partake of the sacrifices; and when the feast of Dionysus came, they were compelled to walk in procession for Dionysus wearing wreaths of ivy.

**6:8** Moreover, a decree went out to the neighboring Greek cities, at the suggestion of Ptolemy, that they should follow the same course against the Jews and make them partake of the sacrifices,

**6:9** and slay those who did not choose to adopt the Greek customs. Thus one might behold the distress that had come upon them.

**6:10** For two women were brought to trial for having circumcised their children; and hanging the infants from their breasts, and parading them publicly around the city, they cast them headlong down from the wall.

**6:11** And others, who had run together into the caves nearby to keep the seventh day secretly, were betrayed to Philip and were all burned together, because out of reverence they were unwilling to defend themselves, for the honor of that most solemn day.

**6:12** Now I urge those who read this book not to be discouraged because of these calamities, but to consider that these punishments were not for the destruction, but for the chastening of our race.

**6:13** For indeed, it is a token of great kindness when those who act ungodly are not left alone for a long time, but immediately fall under penalties.

**6:14** For not as with the other nations, where the Sovereign Lord waits patiently until they have come to the full measure of their sins before He punishes them, has He determined it to be with us;

**6:15** lest, when our sins have come to their full end, He should take vengeance upon us afterward.

**6:16** Therefore He never withdraws His mercy from us; and though He chastens with calamity, He does not forsake His own people.

**6:17** Yet let these things be spoken by us by way of reminder; and briefly we must proceed to the narrative.

**6:18** Eleazar, one of the foremost scribes, a man already advanced in years and most noble in the appearance of his countenance, was forced to open his mouth and eat swine's flesh.

**6:19** But he, welcoming death with honor rather than life with defilement, went forward of his own accord to the rack,

**6:20** having spat it out, coming forward in the manner in which those ought to come who are steadfast to reject what is not lawful to taste, even for the natural love of life.

**6:21** Now those who were appointed over that unlawful sacrificial feast, because of their acquaintance with the man from former times, took him aside privately and appealed to him to bring meat that was lawful for him to use, prepared by himself, and to pretend as though he were eating the meat from the sacrifice appointed by the king,

**6:22** so that by doing this he might be delivered from death, and on account of his longtime friendship with them might receive humane treatment.

**6:23** But he adopted a noble resolve, worthy of his years and of the dignity of his old age, and of his acquired and distinguished way of life, and of his most excellent conduct from childhood—and even more, in harmony with the holy and God-ordained law—and declared his mind accordingly, telling them to send him swiftly to Hades.

**6:24** "For it is not worthy of our age to pretend, lest many of the young, supposing that Eleazar at ninety years of age has gone over to an alien way of life,

**6:25** should themselves also be led astray through me because of my pretense and for the sake of a brief and momentary life, and so I should bring defilement and a stain upon my old age.

**6:26** For even if for the present time I should deliver myself from the punishment of men, yet neither living nor dead shall I escape the hands of the Almighty.

**6:27** Therefore, by manfully parting with this life now, I shall prove myself worthy of my old age,

**6:28** and leave behind to the young a noble example of how to die willingly and nobly for the revered and holy laws." And when he had spoken these words, he went immediately to the rack,

**6:29** while those who led him turned the goodwill they had shown toward him a little while before into ill will, because of the aforementioned words, which they themselves supposed to be madness.

**6:30** And when he was about to die under the blows, he groaned deeply and said, "To the Lord, who has the holy knowledge, it is manifest that, though I might have been delivered from death, I endure harsh pains in my body by being scourged, yet in my soul I gladly suffer these things out of fear of Him."

**6:31** So this man also departed this life in this manner, leaving his death as an example of nobility and a memorial of virtue, not only to the young, but also to the greater part of the nation.

## Translator Notes

- **6:2** — Greek Dios xeniou denotes Zeus Xenius ("Friend" or "Protector of Strangers"). The Greek text reads kathos etynchanon ("just as they were"), referring to the character of the inhabitants, whereas some witnesses read enetynchanon ("as they petitioned").
- **6:19** — Greek tympanon refers to a drum-shaped wooden rack or frame on which victims were stretched and beaten.
- **6:23** — The Greek text reads politeias ("way of life" or "citizenship"), whereas other manuscripts read polias ("gray hair").
- **6:29** — The Greek text concisely omits the participle metabalonton ("turning") found in some witnesses, though the double accusative clearly conveys that his escort turned their earlier goodwill into hostility.
