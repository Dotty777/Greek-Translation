## Text

**13:1** For vain by nature were all men in whom there was ignorance of God, and from the good things that are seen they were not able to know Him who is, nor by giving heed to the works did they recognize the Craftsman.

**13:2** Rather, they supposed either fire, or wind, or swift air, or the circle of the stars, or violent water, or the luminaries of heaven to be gods, the rulers of the world.

**13:3** If, delighting in their beauty, they supposed them to be gods, let them know how much better than these is their Sovereign Master; for the First Author of beauty created them.

**13:4** And if they were astonished at their power and operation, let them perceive from them how much mightier is He who formed them.

**13:5** For from the greatness of beauty and of created things, their Creator is correspondingly beheld.

**13:6** Yet even so, for these men there is little blame; for perhaps they go astray while seeking God and desiring to find Him.

**13:7** For moving among His works they search diligently, and they are persuaded by sight, because the things that are seen are beautiful.

**13:8** Yet on the other hand, not even they are excusable;

**13:9** for if they had power to know so much that they could investigate the world, how did they not sooner find the Sovereign Master of these things?

**13:10** But wretched are they, and among dead things are their hopes, who called the works of men's hands gods—gold and silver, the studied work of craft, and likenesses of animals, or a useless stone, the work of an ancient hand.

**13:11** And if a woodcutting carpenter saws down a tree easy to move, skillfully scrapes off all its bark, and fashioning it becomingly makes a useful vessel for the service of life,

**13:12** and uses up the scraps of his labor to prepare food and eats his fill,

**13:13** and takes the leftover scrap from them that is useful for nothing—a crooked piece of wood grown full of knots—and carves it with the diligence of his leisure, and shapes it by the skill of his idle hours, likening it to the image of a man,

**13:14** or making it resemble some worthless animal, coating it with vermilion and reddening its color with crimson dye, and coating over every blemish that is in it,

**13:15** and having made for it a dwelling worthy of it, sets it in a wall, securing it with iron—

**13:16** so then, he makes provision for it lest it fall down, knowing that it is unable to help itself; for indeed it is an image and has need of help.

**13:17** Yet when he prays concerning his possessions and his marriage and his children, he is not ashamed to speak to that which has no life.

**13:18** And for health he calls upon that which is weak; for life he petitions that which is dead; for aid he implores that which is most inexperienced; for a journey, that which cannot even take a step;

**13:19** and for gain, and for work, and for success of his hands, he asks capability from that which is most incapable with its hands.

## Translator Notes

- **13:1** — The Greek expression ton onta ("Him who is") echoes the Septuagint rendering of the divine name in Exodus 3:14 (ho on, "He who is").
- **13:5** — Follows the Greek text megethous kallones kai ktismaton ("greatness of beauty and of created things"); other manuscripts and editions read megethous kai kallones ktismaton ("greatness and beauty of created things").
- **13:13** — Preserves the Greek oxymoronic pairing en epimeleia argias ("with the diligence of his leisure") and empeiria aneseos ("by the skill of his idle hours"); some witnesses alter aneseos ("relaxation/ease") to syneseos ("understanding").
