## Text

**64:1** For the end; a Psalm of David, a Song.

**64:2** To You a hymn is fitting, O God, in Zion, and to You a vow shall be paid.

**64:3** Give ear to my prayer; to You all flesh shall come.

**64:4** Words of lawless men have overpowered us, and You shall make atonement for our impieties.

**64:5** Blessed is the one whom You have chosen and taken to Yourself; he shall dwell in Your courts. We shall be filled with the good things of Your house; holy is Your temple,

**64:6** wondrous in righteousness. Hear us, O God our Savior, the hope of all the ends of the earth,

**64:7** who establishes mountains by Your strength, being girded with might;

**64:8** who stirs up the water of the sea, the roarings of its waves. The nations shall be troubled,

**64:9** and those who dwell at the utmost bounds shall fear at Your signs; the outgoings of morning and evening You shall gladden.

**64:10** You have visited the earth and drenched it; You have abounded in enriching it; the river of God has been filled with waters; You have prepared their food, for thus is Your preparation.

**64:11** Drench its furrows, multiply its produce; in its raindrops it shall be glad as it springs forth.

**64:12** You shall bless the crown of the year of Your goodness, and Your plains shall be filled with richness;

**64:13** the mountains of the wilderness shall be made rich, and the hills shall gird themselves with exultation.

**64:14** The rams of the sheep have clothed themselves, and the valleys shall abound with grain; they shall shout aloud, for indeed they shall sing hymns.

## Translator Notes

- **64:2** — Greek "is fitting" ("prepei") differs from the Masoretic Text's "awaits" or "silence," and this Greek text ends with "a vow shall be paid" without adding "in Jerusalem."
- **64:6** — The Greek links "wondrous in righteousness" to "Your temple" in the preceding verse and omits the Masoretic Text's phrase concerning the distant seas.
- **64:8** — The Septuagint reads "who stirs up" ("ho syntarasson") the water of the sea, contrasting with the Masoretic Text's "who stills" the roaring of the seas.
- **64:14** — Greek "The rams of the sheep" translates a Hebrew term that can mean either "rams" or "pastures," producing "The rams of the sheep have clothed themselves" rather than "The pastures are clothed with flocks."
