# Psalm 40 — Saints Traditional English (Greek Septuagint)

## Text

**40:1** For the end; a psalm of David.

**40:2** Blessed is he who is mindful of the poor and needy; in an evil day the Lord shall deliver him.

**40:3** May the Lord guard him and count him blessed in the earth, and may He not deliver him into the hands of his enemy.

**40:4** May the Lord help him upon his bed of pain; You have turned his entire bed in his sickness.

**40:5** I said, "O Lord, have mercy on me; heal my soul, for I have sinned against You."

**40:6** My enemies spoke evil of me: "When will he die, and his name perish?"

**40:7** And if he came in to see me, he spoke in vain; his heart gathered iniquity for itself; he went outside and spoke

**40:8** together against me. All my enemies whispered against me; they devised evil against me;

**40:9** they laid down a lawless word against me: "Will he who sleeps not rise up again?"

**40:10** For even the man of my peace, in whom I hoped, who ate my bread, has magnified supplanting against me.

**40:11** But You, O Lord, have mercy on me and raise me up, and I will repay them.

**40:12** By this I have known that You have delighted in me, because my enemy shall by no means rejoice over me.

**40:13** But as for me, because of innocence You have upheld me, and You have established me before You forever.

**40:14** Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, from eternity and to eternity; so be it, so be it.

## Translator Notes

- **40:3** — This Greek text uses optative verbs expressing a blessing and does not include the clause "and keep him alive" found in the Hebrew and some Greek witnesses.
- **40:8** — In the Greek text, "together against me" (epi to auto kat' emou) completes the clause begun at the end of verse 7 ("he went outside and spoke").
- **40:9** — The Greek frames the enemies' taunt as a rhetorical question ("Will he who sleeps not rise up again?", literally "add to rise up"), contrasting with the declarative statement in the Hebrew.
- **40:10** — Greek pternismon ("supplanting" or "tripping up by the heel") renders the Hebrew idiom of lifting the heel in betrayal (compare John 13:18).
- **40:14** — Greek genoito, genoito ("so be it, so be it") renders the closing liturgical "Amen and Amen" at the end of Book I of the Psalms.
