## Text

**22:1** A lazy man is compared to a filthy stone, and everyone will hiss at his dishonor.

**22:2** A lazy man is compared to a lump of dung; everyone who picks him up will shake off his hand.

**22:3** A father's shame is in the birth of an undisciplined son, and a daughter is born to loss.

**22:4** A prudent daughter will inherit her husband, and she who brings shame is a grief to the one who fathered her.

**22:5** An insolent woman shames father and husband, and she will be dishonored by both.

**22:6** Untimely talk is music in mourning, but whips and discipline belong to wisdom at every time.

**22:9** The one who teaches a fool is gluing together a potsherd, waking a sleeper from a deep sleep.

**22:10** The one who talks to a fool is talking to someone dozing off, and at the end he will say, "What is it?"

**22:11** Weep over a dead man, for he lacks light; and weep over a fool, for he lacks understanding. Weep more gently over a dead man, because he has found rest; but the life of the fool is worse than death.

**22:12** Mourning for a dead man is seven days, but for a fool and an ungodly man, all the days of his life.

**22:13** Do not multiply words with a foolish man, and do not go to one without understanding. Be on guard against him, so that you do not have trouble and will by no means be defiled by his spattering. Turn aside from him and you will find rest, and you will by no means grow weary at his madness.

**22:14** What is heavier than lead? And what is its name except a fool?

**22:15** Sand and salt and a lump of iron are easier to bear than a person without understanding.

**22:16** A wooden tie-beam bound into a building will not come apart in an earthquake; so a heart established upon a thought of counsel will not become timid at the time.

**22:17** A heart settled upon a mind of understanding is like a sand-plaster ornament on a smooth wall.

**22:18** Stakes set on a high place will by no means stand firm against the wind; so a timid heart upon a foolish thought will by no means stand firm against any fear.

**22:19** The one who pricks an eye will bring down tears, and one who pricks a heart reveals feeling.

**22:20** One who throws a stone at birds scares them away, and the one who reproaches a friend will dissolve friendship.

**22:21** If you draw a sword against a friend, do not despair, for there is a way back.

**22:22** If you open your mouth against a friend, do not be afraid, for there is reconciliation, except for reproach and arrogance and the revealing of a secret and a treacherous blow; at these things every friend will flee away.

**22:23** Gain trust with your neighbor in poverty, so that you may be filled together in his good things; stay with him in a time of affliction, so that you may be a co-heir in his inheritance.

**22:24** Before fire come the vapor of a furnace and smoke; so before bloodshed come insults.

**22:25** I will not be ashamed to shelter a friend, and I will by no means hide from his face,

**22:26** and if evil things happen to me because of him, everyone who hears will be on guard against him.

**22:27** Who will give me a guard over my mouth and a shrewd seal upon my lips, so that I may not fall from it and my tongue destroy me?

## Translator Notes

- **22:4** — Greek kleronomesei ('will inherit') reflects a Semitic idiom for obtaining or winning a husband for herself.
- **22:6** — Greek sophias is genitive ('of wisdom,' i.e., belonging to wisdom); some witnesses read the nominative sophia ('are wisdom').
- **22:13** — Greek entinagmo is a rare noun meaning 'spattering' or 'shaking off,' picturing filth shaken onto a bystander.
- **22:17** — Greek kosmos psammotos (lit. 'sandy ornament') refers to decorative sand-lime plaster or stucco applied to a smoothly dressed wall.
- **22:27** — Greek ap autes ('from it') is feminine singular, referring either to falling away from the guard or seal, or anticipating 'my tongue' ('because of it').
