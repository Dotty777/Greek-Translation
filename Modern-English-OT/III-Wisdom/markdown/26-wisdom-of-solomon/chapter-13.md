## Text

**13:1** For all people were foolish by nature in whom there was ignorance of God, and from the good things that are seen they were unable to know Him who is, nor by paying attention to the works did they recognize the Craftsman.

**13:2** Instead, they considered either fire, or wind, or swift air, or the circle of stars, or violent water, or the luminaries of heaven—governors of the world—to be gods.

**13:3** And if, delighting in their beauty, they supposed them to be gods, let them know how much better the Master of these is; for the Originator of beauty created them.

**13:4** And if they were astounded by their power and activity, let them understand from them how much more powerful is the One who fashioned them.

**13:5** For from the greatness of beauty and of created things, their Maker is correspondingly perceived.

**13:6** Yet nevertheless, for these there is little blame, for indeed they themselves perhaps go astray while seeking God and wishing to find Him.

**13:7** For living among His works, they search diligently, and they are persuaded by sight, because the things that are seen are beautiful.

**13:8** Yet again, not even they are excusable;

**13:9** for if they were able to know so much that they could investigate the world, how did they not sooner find the Master of these things?

**13:10** But miserable are they, and their hopes are in dead things, who called the works of human hands gods: gold and silver, a product of craft, and likenesses of animals, or a useless stone, the work of an ancient hand.

**13:11** And even if some woodcutting carpenter, having sawn down a tree easy to move, skillfully scraped off all its bark and, crafting it neatly, fashioned a useful vessel for the service of life,

**13:12** and, having used up the scraps of his work for the preparation of food, ate his fill;

**13:13** then, taking a scrap from among them that was useful for nothing—a crooked piece of wood grown full of knots—he carved it in the diligence of his idleness and shaped it with the skill of relaxation; he likened it to the image of a man,

**13:14** or made it like some worthless animal, coating it with vermilion and reddening its color with rouge, and coating over every blemish in it;

**13:15** and having made for it a dwelling worthy of it, he set it in a wall, securing it with iron.

**13:16** Therefore, in order that it would not fall down, he provided for it, knowing that it is unable to help itself; for indeed it is an image and has need of help.

**13:17** Yet when praying about his possessions and marriage and children, he is not ashamed to speak to the lifeless thing.

**13:18** And for health he calls upon what is weak; for life he begs what is dead; for aid he pleads with what is most inexperienced; for a journey, with what is not even able to take a step;

**13:19** and for gain and work and success of his hands, he asks capability from what is most incapable in its hands.

## Translator Notes

- **13:5** — Follows the Greek word order megethous kallones kai ktismaton ("the greatness of beauty and of created things"); other witnesses place the conjunction earlier to read "the greatness and beauty of created things."
- **13:9** — Greek stochasasthai ton aiona literally means "to conjecture" or "to investigate the world" (or "age"), referring to cosmological speculation and calculation.
- **13:11** — The vignette opens with a conditional ei de kai ("And even if") that unfolds through verses 11-15 with aorist verbs in a loose grammatical construction (anacoluthon) characteristic of vivid Hellenistic diatribe.
- **13:13** — Translates the Greek reading aneseos ("relaxation" or "leisure"), paralleling "idleness" (argias) in the previous clause; some manuscripts read syneseos ("understanding" or "skill").
