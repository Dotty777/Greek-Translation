# Proverbs 28 — Saints Modern English (Greek Septuagint)

## Text

**28:1** An ungodly man flees when no one pursues, but a righteous man is confident like a lion.

**28:2** Because of the sins of the ungodly, disputes arise, but a shrewd man will quench them.

**28:3** A man bold in ungodly acts extorts from the poor. Just as a violent and unprofitable rain,

**28:4** so those who forsake the law praise ungodliness; but those who love the law surround themselves with a wall.

**28:5** Evil men will not understand judgment, but those who seek the Lord will understand in everything.

**28:6** Better is a poor man who walks in truth than a false rich man.

**28:7** An understanding son keeps the law, but he who shepherds debauchery dishonors his father.

**28:8** He who multiplies his wealth with interest and excessive gains gathers it for the one who shows mercy to the poor.

**28:9** He who turns away his ear so as not to listen to the law, he himself has made his prayer abominable.

**28:10** He who leads the upright astray on an evil way will himself fall into ruin, and the lawless will pass by good things and will not enter into them.

**28:11** A rich man is wise in his own estimation, but a discerning poor man will condemn him.

**28:12** Because of the help of the righteous great glory comes about, but in the places of the ungodly people are caught.

**28:13** He who covers over his own ungodliness will not prosper, but he who declares reproofs will be loved.

**28:14** Blessed is the man who shrinks back from everything out of reverence, but he who is hard of heart will fall into evils.

**28:15** A hungry lion and a thirsty wolf is one who, being poor, tyrannizes a needy nation.

**28:16** A king lacking revenues is a great extortioner, but he who hates injustice will live a long time.

**28:17** He who goes surety for a man under a charge of murder will be a fugitive and not in safety. Discipline a son and he will love you, and he will give adornment to your soul; you shall by no means obey a lawless nation.

**28:18** He who walks righteously has been helped, but he who walks in crooked ways will be entangled.

**28:19** He who works his own land will be filled with bread, but he who pursues idleness will be filled with poverty.

**28:20** A trustworthy man will be greatly blessed, but the evil man will not go unpunished.

**28:21** He who does not respect the faces of the righteous is not good; such a one will sell a man for a morsel of bread.

**28:22** An envious man hurries to become rich, and he does not know that a merciful man will prevail over him.

**28:23** He who reproves a person's ways will have favors more than the one who flatters with his tongue.

**28:24** He who rejects father or mother and thinks he is not sinning, this one is a partner of an ungodly man.

**28:25** An unbelieving man judges rashly, but he who trusts in the Lord will be cared for.

**28:26** He who trusts in a rash heart, such a one is a fool; but he who walks in wisdom will be saved.

**28:27** He who gives to the poor will not be in need, but he who turns away his eye will be in great distress.

**28:28** In the places of the ungodly the righteous groan, but at their destruction the righteous will be multiplied.

## Translator Notes

- **28:3** — In the Greek syntax, the simile at the end of verse 3 ('Just as a violent and unprofitable rain') directly introduces the main clause at the start of verse 4 ('so those who forsake the law...').
- **28:9** — The Greek uses the transitive perfect middle ebdelyktai with autos as subject and ten proseuchen autou as direct object, meaning 'he himself has made his prayer abominable.'
- **28:15** — The Septuagint refers to a 'thirsty wolf' and a tyrant who is 'poor' (ptochos on), differing from the Hebrew Masoretic Text's 'rushing bear' and 'wicked ruler.'
- **28:17** — In addition to its distinct first couplet about going surety for one charged with murder, the Septuagint includes a second couplet on disciplining a son and refusing to obey a lawless nation.
- **28:22** — Where the Hebrew Masoretic Text warns that 'want' or 'poverty' will come upon the envious man, the Septuagint reads eleemon ('a merciful man'), reflecting a different underlying Hebrew reading.
