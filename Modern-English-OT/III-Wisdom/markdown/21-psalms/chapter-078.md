## Text

**78:1** A psalm for Asaph. God, nations came into Your inheritance; they defiled Your holy temple; they made Jerusalem into a fruit-watcher's hut.

**78:2** They set the corpses of Your servants as food for the birds of the sky, the flesh of Your devout ones for the wild beasts of the earth.

**78:3** They poured out their blood like water around Jerusalem, and there was no one to bury them.

**78:4** We have become a reproach to our neighbors, mockery and scoffing to those around us.

**78:5** How long, Lord, will You be angry to the end? Will Your zeal blaze like fire?

**78:6** Pour out Your wrath upon nations that have not known You, and upon kingdoms that have not called upon Your name.

**78:7** For they have devoured Jacob and made his place desolate.

**78:8** Do not remember our former lawless deeds; may Your compassions quickly come to meet us, for we have become exceedingly poor.

**78:9** Help us, God our Savior, for the sake of the glory of Your name; Lord, deliver us and pardon our sins for the sake of Your name,

**78:10** so that they may never say among the nations, "Where is their God?" And let the avenging of the blood of Your servants that was poured out be made known among the nations before our eyes.

**78:11** Let the groaning of those bound in fetters come before You; according to the greatness of Your arm, preserve the sons of those put to death.

**78:12** Repay our neighbors sevenfold into their lap, their reproach with which they reproached You, Lord.

**78:13** For we, Your people and sheep of Your pasture, will give thanks to You forever; to generation and generation we will proclaim Your praise.

## Translator Notes

- **78:1** — Greek oporophylakion literally means "a fruit-watcher's hut" (a temporary orchard shed), whereas the Hebrew text reads "heaps of ruins."
- **78:10** — The Greek text introduces a negative purpose clause ("so that they may never say among the nations") rather than the Hebrew question "Why should the nations say?"
- **78:11** — Greek tous huious ton tethanatomenon literally means "the sons of those put to death," reflecting a perfect passive participle where the Hebrew idiom "sons of death" refers to those condemned to die.
