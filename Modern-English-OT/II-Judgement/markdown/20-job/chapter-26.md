## Text

**26:1** Then Job answered and said:

**26:2** "To whom are you attached, or whom are you going to help? Is it not the One who has great strength and who has a mighty arm?

**26:3** Whom have you consulted? Is it not the One who has all wisdom? Whom will you follow? Is it not the One who has the greatest power?

**26:4** To whom have you declared words? And whose breath is it that came out from you?

**26:5** Surely giants will not be brought forth from beneath the water and its neighbors?

**26:6** Hades is naked before Him, and there is no covering for destruction.

**26:7** He stretches out the north over nothing, hanging the earth upon nothing.

**26:8** He binds water in His clouds, and no cloud bursts beneath it;

**26:9** He is the One who holds the face of the throne, spreading out His cloud over it.

**26:10** He drew a decree in a circle upon the face of the water until the end of light with darkness.

**26:11** The pillars of heaven took flight and were astounded at His rebuke.

**26:12** By strength He stilled the sea, and by understanding the sea monster has been laid low.

**26:13** And the bars of heaven dread Him; and by a decree He put to death a rebellious dragon.

**26:14** Look, these are parts of His way, and we will hear to the extent of a drop of a word in Him; but the might of His thunder, who knows when He will make it?"

## Translator Notes

- **26:3** — In 26:2-3 the Septuagint reverses the irony of the Hebrew by framing Bildad as aligning with the all-powerful and all-wise God; the middle verb symbebouleusai can mean "consulted" or "counseled."
- **26:5** — Greek gigantes ("giants") renders Hebrew rephaim (shades of the dead), and maiothesontai ("be brought forth") literally refers to delivery as by a midwife.
- **26:11** — Greek epetasthesan can derive either from petomai ("took flight") or from petannymi ("were spread wide"); the former aligns with the startled reaction in the parallel clause.
- **26:12** — Greek to ketos ("the sea monster") translates Hebrew Rahab, and the perfect passive estrotai ("has been laid low") makes the monster the grammatical subject.
- **26:14** — Greek ikmada logou ("a drop of a word") literally means a drop of moisture of a word, and hopote poiesei ("when He will make it") differs from the Hebrew ("who can understand").
