## Text

**4:1** Now the aforementioned Simon, who had become an informer concerning the money and his homeland, was slandering Onias, claiming that it was he who had terrorized Heliodorus and had proved to be the author of the evils.

**4:2** And he dared to call the benefactor of the city, the guardian of his fellow nationals, and a zealot for the laws a plotter against the affairs of state.

**4:3** Now as the hostility progressed to such an extent that murders were even being committed through one of those approved by Simon,

**4:4** Onias, perceiving the severity of the contention and that Apollonius son of Menestheus, the governor of Coele-Phoenicia and Syria, was increasing the wickedness of Simon,

**4:5** prepared himself to go to the king, not becoming an accuser of the citizens, but looking to what was beneficial both publicly and privately for the entire multitude.

**4:6** For he saw that without royal oversight it was impossible to obtain peace for the affairs of state, and that Simon would not cease from his folly.

**4:7** Now when Seleucus departed this life and Antiochus, who was called Epiphanes, received the kingdom, Jason the brother of Onias obtained the high priesthood by corrupt means,

**4:8** promising the king through a petition three hundred sixty talents of silver, and eighty talents from some other revenue.

**4:9** In addition to these, he also promised to pay another one hundred fifty, if it were granted by his authority for him to establish a gymnasium and a youth corps, and to enroll those in Jerusalem as Antiochenes.

**4:10** When the king consented and he gained control of the office, he immediately shifted his fellow countrymen over to the Greek way of life.

**4:11** And setting aside the royal concessions established for the Jews through John the father of Eupolemus, who conducted the embassy for friendship and alliance to the Romans, and abolishing the lawful civic institutions, he introduced unlawful customs.

**4:12** For he gladly established a gymnasium right beneath the citadel itself, and subjecting the noblest of those of our time, he brought them under the broad-brimmed hat.

**4:13** And such was the height of Hellenism and the advance of foreign ways, because of the extreme impurity of the impious Jason, who was no high priest,

**4:14** that the priests were no longer eager for the services of the altar, but despising the temple and neglecting the sacrifices, they hurried to take part in the unlawful exhibition in the wrestling arena after the summons of the discus,

**4:15** and counting ancestral honors as nothing, while considering Greek glories the finest.

**4:16** Because of these things as well, harsh adversity surrounded them, and those very people whose ways of life they emulated and whom they wished to resemble in every respect, they had as their enemies and punishers.

**4:17** For to act impiously against the divine laws is no light matter; rather, the time that follows will make these things clear.

**4:18** Now when a five-year contest was being held in Tyre and the king was present,

**4:19** the vile Jason sent envoys, as Antiochenes from Jerusalem, carrying three hundred drachmas of silver for the sacrifice to Heracles, which even those who carried them requested not be used for a sacrifice,

**4:20** but on account of those present, for the construction of the triremes.

**4:21** Now when Apollonius son of Menestheus was sent to Egypt for the enthronement of King Philometor, Antiochus, learning that he was hostile to his established affairs, took thought for his own security; therefore, coming to Joppa, he arrived in Jerusalem.

**4:22** Magnificently welcomed by Jason and the city, he was received in with torchlight and shouts; and after that he marched into Phoenicia to encamp.

**4:23** After a period of three years, Jason sent Menelaus, the brother of the previously mentioned Simon, to carry the money to the king and to complete memoranda concerning necessary affairs.

**4:24** But he, having been introduced to the king and having extolled him with the appearance of authority, diverted the high priesthood to himself, outbidding Jason by three hundred talents of silver.

**4:25** Having received the royal mandates, he arrived, bringing nothing worthy of the high priesthood, but having the passions of a cruel tyrant and the rages of a savage beast.

**4:26** And Jason, who had corruptly supplanted his own brother, being corruptly supplanted by another, had been driven as a fugitive into the land of Ammanitis.

**4:27** Meanwhile, Menelaus held control of the office, yet he made no regular payment of the money promised to the king,

**4:28** even though Sostratus, the commander of the citadel, kept demanding payment (for to him belonged the collection of the revenues); for which reason the two of them were summoned by the king.

**4:29** And Menelaus left behind his own brother Lysimachus as deputy of the high priesthood, while Sostratus left Crates, who was over the Cypriots.

**4:30** While such matters were taking place, it happened that the people of Tarsus and Mallus revolted, because they were being given as a gift to Antiochis, the king's concubine.

**4:31** Therefore the king went hastily to settle affairs, leaving behind as his deputy Andronicus, one of those in high rank.

**4:32** Now Menelaus, thinking that he had gained a favorable opportunity, stole some of the gold vessels of the temple and presented them as gifts to Andronicus, while others he had already sold to Tyre and the surrounding cities.

**4:33** And when Onias clearly learned of this, he reported it, having withdrawn to a place of sanctuary at Daphne, which lies near Antioch.

**4:34** Therefore Menelaus, taking Andronicus aside privately, urged him to kill Onias. So he came to Onias, and having been persuaded to deceit and pledging friendship with oaths, giving his right hand, even though he lay under suspicion, he persuaded him to come out from the sanctuary; and immediately he dispatched him, showing no respect for justice.

**4:35** For this reason, not only Jews, but also many of the other nations were indignant and distressed at the unjust murder of the man.

**4:36** When the king returned from the regions of Cilicia, the Jews in the city appealed to him, with the Greeks also sharing their hatred of the wickedness, because Onias had been killed without reason.

**4:37** Therefore Antiochus, grieved to his soul, moved to pity, and weeping because of the self-control and great orderly conduct of the one who had departed,

**4:38** and inflamed with fury, immediately stripped off Andronicus's purple robe, tore off his tunics, led him around through the whole city to the very place where he had acted impiously against Onias, and there put the murderer out of the way, as the Lord repaid him the punishment he deserved.

**4:39** Now when many acts of temple robbery had been committed in the city by Lysimachus with the consent of Menelaus, and the report had spread abroad, the multitude gathered together against Lysimachus, for many gold vessels had already been carried away.

**4:40** Now as the crowds were being stirred up and filled with rage, Lysimachus armed about three thousand men and initiated unjust violence, led by a certain Auranus, a man advanced in age and no less so in ignorance.

**4:41** When they also perceived the attack of Lysimachus, some snatched up stones, others thick pieces of wood, and some grabbed handfuls of the ashes lying nearby, and hurled them in confusion at those around Lysimachus.

**4:42** For this reason, they left many of them wounded, struck down some, and drove all of them to flight, while the temple-robber himself they killed beside the treasury.

**4:43** Concerning these matters, a trial was instituted against Menelaus.

**4:44** When the king arrived in Tyre, the three men sent by the council of elders presented the case before him.

**4:45** Already defeated, Menelaus promised considerable money to Ptolemy son of Dorymenes to persuade the king.

**4:46** Therefore Ptolemy took the king aside into a certain colonnade, as if for him to cool off, and changed his mind.

**4:47** And Menelaus, the cause of the entire wickedness, he acquitted of the charges, while for those wretched men, who even if they had spoken before Scythians would have been acquitted as blameless, for them he decreed death.

**4:48** Quickly, therefore, those who had spoken on behalf of the city, the peoples, and the sacred vessels suffered the unjust penalty.

**4:49** For this reason, even the Tyrians, hating the wickedness, magnificently provided what was needed for their funeral.

**4:50** But Menelaus, because of the greed of those in power, remained in office, growing in wickedness, having proved to be a great plotter against the citizens.

## Translator Notes

- **4:4** — The Greek text reads "Coele-Phoenicia and Syria" (as in Codex Alexandrinus), whereas other witnesses read the more usual "Coele-Syria and Phoenicia."
- **4:5** — The Greek text reads diekosmethe ("prepared himself [to go]," as in Codex Alexandrinus), whereas other witnesses read diekomisthe ("made his way" or "traveled").
- **4:12** — The Greek text reads ton eph' hemon ("those of our time," as in Codex Alexandrinus), whereas other witnesses read ton ephebon ("the youths/ephebes"). "The broad-brimmed hat" translates petason, the Greek hat worn by ephebes and associated with Hermes.
- **4:20** — The Greek text preserves a shorter reading (as in Codex Alexandrinus) that omits intervening clauses found in other witnesses and reads "on account of those present" (ton paronton) rather than "on account of the bearers" (ton parakomizonton).
- **4:33** — The Greek text reads apenegken ("reported it" or "brought charges," as in Codex Alexandrinus), whereas other witnesses read apelegchen ("exposed" or "rebuked him").
