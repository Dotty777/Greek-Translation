## Text

**6:1** Not long afterward the king sent out an elderly Athenian to compel the Jews to depart from the ancestral laws and not to live by the laws of God,

**6:2** and also to defile the temple in Jerusalem and call it by the name of Olympian Zeus, and the one at Gerizim, just as those who inhabited the place happened to be, by that of Zeus the Friend of Strangers.

**6:3** And the onslaught of evil was harsh and altogether hard to bear.

**6:4** For the temple was filled with debauchery and revelry by the Gentiles, who dallied with prostitutes and had intercourse with women in the sacred areas of the precincts, and moreover brought inside things that were not fitting.

**6:5** And the altar was filled with unlawful things prohibited by the laws.

**6:6** And it was possible neither to keep the Sabbath, nor to observe the ancestral festivals, nor simply to confess oneself to be a Jew.

**6:7** And with bitter compulsion they led them on the monthly birthday of the king to the eating of sacrifices; and when the festival of Dionysus occurred, they were forced to march in procession for Dionysus wearing ivy.

**6:8** And a decree went out to the neighboring Greek cities, at the suggestion of Ptolemy, to carry out the same policy against the Jews and to have them eat of the sacrifices,

**6:9** and to slaughter those who did not choose to pass over to Greek ways. One could therefore see the present misery.

**6:10** For two women were brought up for having circumcised their children; and having hung the infants from their breasts and led them publicly around the city, they hurled them down from the wall.

**6:11** And others, having run together into the nearby caves to keep the seventh day secretly, when betrayed to Philip, were burned to death together, because they had scruples against defending themselves out of regard for the glory of that most revered day.

**6:12** Therefore I urge those who read this book not to be disheartened because of the calamities, but to consider that the punishments are not for destruction, but for the discipline of our race.

**6:13** For indeed, that those who act impiously are not left alone for a long time, but immediately meet with penalties, is a sign of great kindness.

**6:14** For unlike with the other nations, where the Master waits patiently to punish them until they have reached the full measure of their sins, He did not decide for it to be so with us,

**6:15** so that He would not later take vengeance on us when our sins have reached their end.

**6:16** Therefore He never withdraws His mercy from us; and though He disciplines with calamity, He does not forsake His own people.

**6:17** However, let these things be said by us by way of reminder; and we must proceed briefly to the narrative.

**6:18** Eleazar, one of the foremost scribes, a man already advanced in age and most noble in the appearance of his face, was being forced to open his mouth wide and eat pork.

**6:19** But he, welcoming death with honor rather than life with pollution, went forward of his own accord to the rack,

**6:20** having spat it out, in the manner in which those ought to come forward who have the resolve to reject things that it is not lawful to taste for love of life.

**6:21** Now those who were appointed over the unlawful eating of sacrifices, because of their acquaintance with the man from former times, took him aside privately and called upon him to bring meat that it was proper for him to use, prepared by himself, and to pretend to be eating the portions of the sacrificial meat commanded by the king,

**6:22** so that by doing this he might be released from death, and because of his old friendship with them might obtain humane treatment.

**6:23** But he, adopting a noble resolve worthy of his age and of the dignity of his old age and of his acquired and distinguished way of life and of his most excellent conduct from childhood, and still more in accordance with the holy and God-established legislation, declared himself, telling them to send him quickly to Hades:

**6:24** "For it is not worthy of our age to pretend, so that many of the young, supposing that ninety-year-old Eleazar has gone over to foreign ways,

**6:25** should themselves, because of my pretense and for the sake of this brief and fleeting life, be led astray through me, and I should acquire pollution and a stain upon my old age.

**6:26** For even if for the present I should escape the punishment from men, yet neither alive nor dead will I escape the hands of the Almighty.

**6:27** Therefore, by courageously departing life now, I will show myself worthy of my old age,

**6:28** and will have left to the young a noble example of how to die a good death willingly and nobly for the revered and holy laws." And having said these things, he immediately went to the rack,

**6:29** while these men turned their goodwill toward him of a little earlier into ill will because of the madness, as they supposed, of the words just spoken.

**6:30** And when he was about to die from the blows, he groaned aloud and said, "To the Lord who has holy knowledge it is clear that though I could be released from death, I endure severe pains in my body as I am scourged, yet in my soul I gladly suffer these things because of fear of Him."

**6:31** So in this manner this man also departed life, leaving his own death not only for the young, but also for the majority of the nation, as an example of nobility and a memorial of virtue.

## Translator Notes

- **6:1** — Greek geronta Athenaion may mean "an elderly Athenian," "an Athenian senator," or a proper name, "Geron the Athenian."
- **6:2** — Greek Dios xeniou ("Zeus Xenios") refers to Zeus as patron of hospitality and strangers. The Greek text reads etynchanon ("happened to be"), linking the title to the character of the inhabitants; other manuscripts read enetynchanon ("petitioned" or "requested").
- **6:19** — Greek tympanon refers to a drum-shaped wheel or wooden rack on which victims were stretched and beaten to death.
- **6:23** — The Greek text reads politeias ("way of life" or "citizenship"); other manuscripts read polias ("gray hair").
- **6:29** — The Greek text uses a compact construction (agonton ... eumeneian dysmeneian, literally "bringing goodwill [to] ill will"); other manuscripts expand this explicitly with eis ... metabalonton ("turning goodwill into ill will").
