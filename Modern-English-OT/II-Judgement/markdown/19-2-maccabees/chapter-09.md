## Text

**9:1** Now about that time Antiochus happened to have retreated in disorder from the regions around Persia.

**9:2** For he had entered the city called Persepolis and attempted to rob the temples and hold down the city. Therefore, when the crowds rushed forward, they turned to the aid of weapons; and it happened that Antiochus, routed by the local inhabitants, made a disgraceful withdrawal.

**9:3** Now while he was at Ecbatana, word reached him of what had happened concerning Nicanor and those with Timothy.

**9:4** Carried away with fury, he thought to vent upon the Jews even the wrong of those who had put him to flight. Therefore he ordered the charioteer to complete the journey by driving without ceasing, though indeed the judgment from heaven was accompanying him. For thus he spoke arrogantly: "I will make Jerusalem a common burial ground of Jews when I arrive there."

**9:5** But the all-seeing Lord, the God of Israel, struck him with an incurable and invisible blow. As soon as he had finished the speech, an incurable pain of the bowels seized him, and bitter torments of his internal parts,

**9:6** most justly, since he had tormented the bowels of others with many strange afflictions.

**9:7** Yet he in no way ceased from his insolence, and moreover he was filled with arrogance, breathing fire in his fury against the Jews and commanding the journey to be hastened. And it even happened that he fell from the chariot as it rushed along at headlong speed, and suffering a severe fall, all the limbs of his body were wrenched out of joint.

**9:8** And the one who presumed against the waves of the sea because of his superhuman arrogance, and supposed he would weigh the heights of the mountains in a balance, was brought down to the ground and carried along in a litter, displaying the power of God plainly to all,

**9:9** so that worms even seethed out of the body of the impious man, and while he was still alive in agonies and pains, his flesh fell away, and because of his stench the whole army was weighed down by the decay.

**9:10** And no one was able to carry along the one who a little earlier thought he could touch the heavenly stars, because of the unbearable burden of the stench.

**9:11** At this point, then, broken as he was, he began to abandon much of his arrogance and to come to his senses under the divine scourge, his pains intensifying moment by moment.

**9:12** And not even being able to endure his own stench, he said these words: "It is right to be subject to God, and for one who is mortal not to think arrogant thoughts."

**9:13** Then the abominable man made a vow to the Master who would no longer have mercy on him, saying as follows:

**9:14** that the holy city, which he had been hurrying to come and make level with the ground and build into a common burial ground, he would declare free;

**9:15** and as for the Jews, since he had resolved not even to consider them worthy of burial, to destroy them along with their infants for wild beasts as food for birds, he would make all of them equal to Athenians;

**9:16** and the holy temple which he had formerly plundered he would adorn with the finest votive offerings, and he would restore all the sacred vessels many times over, and he would supply from his own revenues the expenses required for the sacrifices;

**9:17** and in addition to these things, he would also become a Jew and would visit every inhabited place, proclaiming the might of God.

**9:18** But when his pains in no way ceased, for the righteous judgment of God had come upon him, despairing of his own condition, he wrote to the Jews the letter written below, having the form of a supplication and containing these words:

**9:19** "To the worthy Jews, the citizens, hearty greetings, good health, and prosperity, from Antiochus, king and general.

**9:20** Be well, you and your children; and your own affairs will be according to your wish. Having my hope in heaven,

**9:21** I affectionately remembered your honor and goodwill. Having returned from the regions of Persia and fallen into an illness involving hardship, I considered it necessary to take thought for the common security of you all,

**9:22** not despairing of my own condition, but having great hope of escaping the illness.

**9:23** Yet considering that my father also, at the times when he campaigned in the upper regions, appointed his successor,

**9:24** so that if anything unexpected should happen and anything grievous should also go forth, those throughout the country, knowing to whom the affairs of state have been left, might not be disturbed;

**9:25** and in addition to these things, observing that the neighboring rulers and those bordering on the kingdom are watching for opportunities and awaiting what will happen, I have appointed my son Antiochus as king, whom I often entrusted and commended to most of you when hurrying up to the upper satrapies. And I have written to him what is written below.

**9:26** Therefore I urge and ask you, remembering the benefits both collectively and individually, that each one preserve his existing goodwill toward me and my son;

**9:27** for I am persuaded that he, aligned with my policy, will conduct himself gently and humanely toward you."

**9:28** So the murderer and blasphemer, having suffered the worst things just as he had treated others, ended his life by a most pitiable fate in a foreign land, in the mountains.

**9:29** And Philip, his foster brother, conveyed the body back; and fearing the son of Antiochus, he crossed over to Ptolemy Philometor in Egypt.

## Translator Notes

- **9:8** — The Greek text reads "presumed against the waves of the sea" (antidokon tois tes thalasses kumasin, as in Codex Alexandrinus), whereas other witnesses read "just now thought he could command the waves of the sea" (arti dokon ... epitassein).
- **9:12** — The Greek text reads "think arrogant thoughts" (hyperephana phronein, as in Codex Alexandrinus), whereas other witnesses read "think himself equal to God" (isothea phronein).
- **9:15** — The Greek text reads "to destroy" (ektripsai, as in Codex Alexandrinus), where other witnesses read "to cast out" (ekripsai).
- **9:20** — The Greek text reads "Be well ... and your own affairs will be" (as in Codex Alexandrinus), omitting the conditional "if" and the clause "we give the greatest thanks" found in other witnesses.
- **9:24** — The Greek text reads "should also go forth" (prosapelthe, as in Codex Alexandrinus), where other witnesses read "should be reported" (prosangelthe).
