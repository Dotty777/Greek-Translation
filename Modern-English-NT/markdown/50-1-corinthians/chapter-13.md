## Text

**13:1** If I speak in the tongues of people and of angels, but do not have love, I have become resounding bronze or a clashing cymbal.

**13:2** And if I have prophecy and understand all the mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith so as to move mountains, but do not have love, I am nothing.

**13:3** And if I dole out all my possessions, and if I hand over my body so that I may boast, but do not have love, I gain nothing.

**13:4** Love is patient, love is kind, love does not envy, does not brag, is not puffed up,

**13:5** does not behave improperly, does not seek its own, is not provoked, does not keep count of wrong,

**13:6** does not rejoice over unrighteousness, but rejoices together with the truth;

**13:7** it bears up under all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things.

**13:8** Love never fails. But whether there are prophecies, they will be brought to an end; whether tongues, they will cease; whether knowledge, it will be brought to an end.

**13:9** For in part we know, and in part we prophesy;

**13:10** but when what is complete comes, what is in part will be brought to an end.

**13:11** When I was a child, I spoke like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child; when I became a man, I put an end to the things of a child.

**13:12** For at present we see through a mirror indistinctly, but then face to face; at present I know in part, but then I will know fully, just as I also was fully known.

**13:13** But now faith, hope, love remain, these three; and the greatest of these is love.

## Translator Notes

- **13:3** — The Greek text reads ἵνα καυχήσωμαι ('so that I may boast'), supported by early Alexandrian witnesses (P46, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus); other manuscripts read καυθήσωμαι ('so that I may be burned').
- **13:10** — Greek τὸ τέλειον ('what is complete' or 'the perfect') contrasts directly with τὸ ἐκ μέρους ('what is in part') and is widely understood as the full maturity realized at Christ's return.
- **13:12** — Greek ἐν αἰνίγματι literally means 'in a riddle' (cf. Numbers 12:8 LXX), conveying an obscure or indistinct reflection as viewed in an ancient metal mirror.
